Case study 01 · Wayground (formerly Quizizz)

Why teachers skip a legal mandate

Accommodations are not optional. They are written into student IEPs and enforced by law, and failing to deliver them is the single most common trigger for due process against a school. Yet on platform, accommodations were barely being switched on. I spent nine months embedded in the accommodations pod finding out why, and building what closed the gap.

RoleSole UX Researcher, accommodations pod
Duration9 to 10 months
Participants47+ across studies and survey
ShippedCo-teacher flow, reporting, accommodation features
01 · The paradox

Legally required, rarely used

The pod was handed a set of usage patterns that did not add up. Every one of them pointed somewhere, but none of them explained themselves.

Anomaly 01

Accommodations were set far more often in asynchronous activities than in live ones, even though live classroom use was higher overall.

Anomaly 02

Elementary teachers used accommodations more than secondary teachers, despite secondary classrooms carrying comparable IEP loads.

Anomaly 03

English classrooms showed the highest usage by subject, well ahead of math and science.

Anomaly 04

Teachers with the heaviest overall platform usage were often not setting up accommodations at all, which inverted the expected relationship.

02 · The explanation

Asking teachers what the data could not say

Twelve semi-structured interviews paired with a 35 response survey, covering general education teachers, special education teachers, and co-taught classrooms.

  • Live activities leave no room. Setting up accommodations mid-lesson competes with running the lesson, so teachers default to skipping them when the class is live.
  • Students opt out of visible support. Read aloud and separate testing get refused because peers notice. Teachers switch off accommodations their students find embarrassing.
  • Plan limitations blocked access. Some accommodations sat behind tiers teachers did not have, so the feature simply was not available at the moment of need.
  • Subject shaped need. English classrooms leaned on read aloud and extended time far more than math, which explained the subject skew rather than any difference in teacher diligence.
03 · The structural finding

The person who knows is not the person with access

Underneath the behavioural explanations sat something structural, and it reframed the entire problem for the pod.

21% / 31%

Only 21% of special education teachers were setting up accommodations on platform. Meanwhile 31% of general education teachers had a SPED teacher in their classroom and were doing the setup themselves, secondhand, without holding the IEP knowledge.

  • IEP knowledge lives with the SPED teacher. They hold the legal document, the student history, and the specific requirements.
  • Platform access lives with the class owner. Usually the general education teacher, who often did not have the detail.
  • Accommodations were built at class level. But students exist across classes, so a student's accommodations had to be rebuilt every time, in every class, by whoever happened to own it.
  • Co-taught classrooms had no shared model. Resource rooms, self-contained classrooms, and push-in support all worked differently, and the product recognised none of those differences.
✓ Shipped · Co-teacher flow
04 · The second job

Delivering an accommodation is only half of it

A finding that surfaced repeatedly and changed what the product needed to be. Teachers were not only required to provide accommodations. They were required to prove they had.

  • Teachers were screenshotting quizzes and filing them into physical binders as evidence.
  • Parallel spreadsheets tracked which accommodation was given to which student, and when.
  • Documentation consumed a significant share of the working week, reported by teachers at around a fifth of their time.
  • Proof was produced on fixed cadences, commonly three, five, and ten week reporting cycles, and on demand whenever a parent asked.
  • An accommodation delivered but undocumented did not satisfy the mandate, which meant delivery alone was never the job.
✓ Shipped · Exportable accommodations reporting
05 · The dignity layer

Accommodations students will actually accept

The refusal finding had a product answer. If visible support gets rejected, support has to stop being visible.

Speech to text

Gave students an input path that did not mark them out to the room, and removed the need for an adult to sit beside them reading aloud.

Reduced visual clutter

Built for students who needed a calmer screen, and usable by anyone, so turning it on carried no signal.

Testing mode

Brought accommodations into assessment contexts, where the compliance stakes are highest and the tolerance for improvisation is lowest.

Why this mattered

Every one of these lowered the social cost of using an accommodation, which was the actual blocker behind a measurable share of switch-offs.

06 · The JTBD thread

Which accommodations matter, and when

One of the last studies I ran on the pod mapped accommodations against the instructional cycle rather than treating them as a flat list of settings.

  • Eight interviews mapping pre-instruction, instruction, practice, review, and assessment as distinct phases with distinct needs.
  • Some accommodations are essential during review and assessment, and actively counterproductive during skill building, where the point is productive struggle.
  • This produced a recommendation for a wean-off plan, letting support taper deliberately as a student gains independence rather than staying static all year.
  • The same lens carried directly into my next project, where JTBD research established where a math practice product belonged in a teacher's week.
07 · Impact

What moved

+8pp

Increase in accommodations adoption over a single trimester, following the co-teacher flow and the accommodation features shipped from this research.

Confirm exact metric definition and window before publishing
  • Co-teacher flow gave SPED teachers direct access, so the person holding the IEP could act on it.
  • Exportable reporting turned the documentation burden into a product feature rather than a binder.
  • Speech to text, reduced clutter, and testing mode addressed the refusal problem at its source.
  • A shared model of co-taught classrooms that the pod continued designing against after I moved on.
08 · Also in those nine months

The work around the work

District and school compliance research

Mapped which offices oversee special education, how district level oversight actually reaches a classroom, and where accountability sits when one administrator covers many schools. I scoped, designed, and recruited this study, then handed it over when I moved to the VoyageMath product.

Differentiation frameworks, built with GenAI

Reverse engineered subject specific differentiation frameworks by synthesising academic studies, public documentation, practitioner forums, and teacher shared artifacts. The volume of source material made this impractical by hand, and generative AI is what made the synthesis feasible at that scale.

A standing SPED teacher community

Hosted recurring open floor sessions with special education teachers rather than recruiting fresh for every study. That relationship became research infrastructure the whole pod drew on.

Secondary research foundation

Built the pod's baseline understanding of IEP law, due process risk, and how accommodations are mandated and audited, so product conversations started from the legal reality rather than from assumptions.

Case study 02 · Wayground (formerly Quizizz)

Rebuilding a math practice funnel

VoyageMath had teachers arriving and then quietly disappearing. Four studies traced the drop-off from first impression to daily use, and the last one turned into an argument with engineering that I had the evidence to win.

RoleSole UX Researcher
ScopeOnboarding, email, JTBD, reports page
Participants35 to 40 middle school math teachers
Shipped3 products
01 · Onboarding

Teachers could not tell if it was built for them

Usability interviews with six teachers found the flow prioritised content browsing over the information teachers needed to decide whether to adopt at all.

  • Grade level and standards coverage, the single biggest decision factor, did not appear in the first two screens.
  • The step by step guide, a core differentiator, only surfaced on the Assign page, long after the adoption decision was made.
  • The adaptive question bank was not legible from the preview, so its main advantage went unnoticed.
  • The onboarding felt structurally separate from the product itself, creating a jarring handoff.
✓ Shipped · Redesigned onboarding flow
02 · Post-review email

Turning a finished review into a reason to come back

Two phases, four discovery interviews and eight usability sessions, testing three live prototypes. I owned this end to end: research design, copy, information architecture, and visual design.

  • Brand confusion. Leading with the VoyageMath logo made teachers unsure which platform had sent the email or where the original activity lived.
  • Subject lines. Direct, classroom relevant phrasing referencing review results outperformed everything else tested.
  • Standards versus questions. The split was even at four each, but when shown a mixed layout, three of the four standards-first teachers switched preference.
  • Calls to action. Multiple CTAs created hesitation, because teachers could not predict where any of them led.
✓ Shipped · Post-review email, owned end to end
03 · Jobs to be done

Where the product actually fits in a teacher's week

Eight interviews with high usage teachers, mapping the full instructional cycle rather than asking about features.

  • Wayground's job was reviews and test prep, short quizzes, bell ringers, and fun games.
  • VoyageMath's job was independent practice immediately after a skill is taught, and targeted intervention for students needing foundational support.
  • The step guide becomes critical the first time a student solves alone, because it is the only support present in that moment.
  • Unit reviews before tests span multiple standards, which made them the natural moment to surface targeted practice.
04 · The advocacy win

Holding the line on pre-selection

Testing the reports page touchpoint with six to seven teachers, evaluating an assign flow where struggling students would be pre-selected automatically.

4 studies. 35-40 teachers. One answer.

Engineering flagged architecture constraints that made pre-selection difficult, and the easier path was a generic assign flow with manual selection. I pushed back, because this was not a single study's finding. Across four separate studies and roughly forty teacher conversations, teachers consistently wanted the tool to do the identification work rather than making them repeat it. That consistency is what got pre-selection built.

✓ Shipped · Reports page surfacing with pre-selection
05 · Reflection

What I would do differently

What worked

Anchoring every study to a specific funnel stage and a measurable metric made the research feel necessary rather than optional. Naming exactly where teachers lost confidence was far more actionable than general usability themes.

What I would change

I would have pushed for post-launch measurement on the onboarding redesign sooner. The pre-launch findings were rich, but tracking activation after ship came late, and closing that loop earlier would have made the whole chain provable.

Case study 02
Research practice

Building research capacity, not just reports

A study answers one question. The things below were built so the team could keep answering questions after the study ended, and so that findings did not die inside a document nobody reopened.

01 · The distributed question

Three researchers, one shared dataset

Each of us was running separate studies with separate goals. But we all had teachers on calls every week, and we were each spending that access on our own questions only.

  • We agreed on one shared question per cycle, unrelated to any individual study.
  • Each researcher appended it to their own sessions, costing a few minutes per call.
  • Pooled across three researchers and a full cycle, that produced a dataset none of us could have justified running a dedicated study to collect.
Start of day, and again at 9pm

One cycle asked when teachers actually do administrative work. The answer: heavily at the start of the day, some at the end, almost never in the middle, and a second window at home after dinner. That reshaped assumptions about when to send anything time sensitive.

02 · The repository

Secondary research that starts with our own work

Past research was scattered across documents that were effectively unsearchable once the project ended, so teams re-ran questions that had already been answered.

  • Built a repository of past studies, indexed by insight rather than by project name or date.
  • Made it queryable, so a researcher beginning secondary research could ask whether a question had already come up and where.
  • Used generative AI for the categorisation and retrieval layer, which is what made insight level indexing feasible rather than aspirational.
  • The practical effect was fewer redundant studies and faster synthesis at the start of every new project.
03 · Teaching as recruitment

Sessions that gave something back

Testing with students is hard to recruit for and easy to make extractive. We restructured the visit so it ran in both directions.

  • Went into schools and taught students something real, covering design thinking, career paths, and how products get built.
  • Ran product testing in the same visit, with participants who were engaged rather than processed.
  • Schools said yes more readily, because the visit was worth their time independent of our research needs.
04 · Making findings travel

Newsletter and dogfooding

Monthly research newsletter

Wrote and shipped editions summarising what the research team had learned that month, so findings reached people who were never in the readout and would never open the full report.

Dogfooding

Used the product as our users did, on a regular cadence, which surfaced friction that structured studies were not designed to catch.

M.S. by Research, Computational Humanities · IIIT Hyderabad

Music as a social surrogate

Music vs. Social Media as Social Surrogates: Healthy and Unhealthy Use During India's Second COVID-19 Wave. A quantitative psychometric study run during the enforced isolation of the second wave, testing whether the medium people reached for actually mattered.

DegreesM.S. by Research · B.Tech Computer Science
InstitutionIIIT Hyderabad
StatusExtended abstracts published, full paper in preparation
01 · Method

What the study actually did

  • Compared music and social media as social surrogates, and separated healthy use from unhealthy use rather than measuring volume.
  • Adapted an existing music use framework to social media as an original instrument, then tested its reliability through factor analysis.
  • Measured personality using the Big Five model to test whether vulnerability was person level or medium level.
  • Tested six directional hypotheses against data collected during the crisis itself, not retrospectively.
02 · Findings

The expected answer did not survive

  • Music outperformed social media as a source of comfort and social surrogacy overall.
  • The advantage was strongest on personal needs such as comfort, reminiscence, and sitting with isolation.
  • Social media's presumed community advantage did not hold up statistically, which was the result the study was designed to find and did not.
  • Unhealthy use correlated across both media. People who used one unhealthily tended to use the other unhealthily too.
  • Healthy use did not correlate across media, which located the problem in the person rather than the platform.
  • Neuroticism significantly predicted unhealthy use of both, supporting a person level vulnerability.
