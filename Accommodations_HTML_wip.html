Chapter 02 · The mismatch, named

Co-teacher research

This is also where the actual IEP lifecycle got mapped for the first time. It starts with an ARD meeting (Admission, Review, Dismissal), attended by the general education teacher, the special ed teacher, a counselor, other service providers, and the parents. That's where the IEP is finalized and becomes a legal document.

Walked in asking

Who are these co-teachers, and what does the control/responsibility mismatch actually look like in practice?

What we did

A survey went out first and surfaced the split below. 12 semi-structured interviews followed, to explain why it existed.

The survey found the pattern. The interviews explained it: the workaround wasn't occasional, it was the norm. Two things came directly out of this: a co-teacher flow, so SPED teachers could set accommodations without borrowing someone else's account, and an exportable reporting feature, a way to prove accommodations were actually delivered, which phase 4 showed was its own unmet need.

How it works

Any teacher can add another teacher as a co-teacher on their class. The co-teacher gets access to that classroom, and can switch between every classroom they've been added to, across different teachers and subjects.

The part that answers the research directly

If a co-teacher sets an accommodation for a student in one subject, it now carries over automatically to every other subject where they co-teach that same student. This is the exact thing 11 of 15 SPED teachers asked for in chapter one: accommodations that persist across subjects and co-teachers, not re-entered every time.

Reporting

Co-teachers get full visibility on the report side too: they can view and download exactly what accommodations were given and complied with, across every classroom they're part of.

Driving adoption

Shipping the flow didn't guarantee it got used. An invite email asking someone to click and add a co-teacher only works if it lands when they actually have a free moment. So the team ran a fast, low-cost study to find out when that moment actually is.

~75
teachers answered one shared question in a single week, added to every researcher's interviews already in progress: "when do you actually have time for admin work like this?"
2
main windows emerged: first thing in the morning, before class starts, and again at the end of the day. Some teachers took it home instead.

The more useful finding was underneath that: the pattern often clustered by school or district, not just by individual habit, as if a whole school operated on the same rhythm. Co-teacher invite emails were retimed to these windows instead of sent generically, with an in-app reminder for teachers who saw the prompt but didn't have the two minutes right then.

Chapter 03 · The open thread

District and school level

Walked in asking

If compliance is the real weight behind all of this, who actually answers for it when it fails?

What we did

Secondary research first, not a stack of published studies but internal conversations with customer success teams, conversations with SPED teachers themselves, and outside education data, triangulated to answer a question nobody had mapped before: which offices exist at the school and district level, and how directly or indirectly involved each one actually is.

What we found, at the school level

Three roles: the Vice Principal, the SPED clerk or coordinator, and the school counselor. Which one is actually in charge depends on the school.

Broke open

Making sure a student got their accommodations was never just on the classroom teacher, or even the SPED teacher. There's a whole chain of people above them responsible for it too, people the team had a rough sense of but had never actually mapped. That's what broke open: finding out who they are, why it matters to each of them, and how the product could actually help them do that job.

Authority sits at the top, furthest from daily delivery. Accountability sits in the middle. Delivery sits near the bottom, closest to what the product touches. The open question by the time this thread was handed off: if someone at the district level is the one responsible for accommodations happening across every school, how does the product actually help them do that?

Synthesis

One mismatch, showing up at every level

Under-adoption was never one problem. It was the same control/responsibility split repeating at three scales: in the classroom, between co-teachers, and up through the chain of accountability to the district. The resolution wasn't a single feature. It was a co-teacher flow, an exportable proof-of-accommodation record, and a research thread on district accountability that's still open.

What shipped, and what changed

THE IMPACT UNDERNEATH THE IMPACT
Before this thread, special education was a legal checklist inside the product. Three studies over two months built a working, end-to-end understanding instead.
  • IEP creation: ARD meeting decides accommodations, makes it legal.
  • Chain of command: accountability sits furthest from the classroom.
  • Classroom types: four models, four different accountability setups.
  • Workload: 57-hour weeks, up to 20 spent on paperwork.
  • Records: tracked by hand, six to eight hours weekly.
  • What matters: quiet vigilance over work that isn't theirs.
Shipped

Co-teacher flow with cross-subject persistence, so an accommodation set once follows the student everywhere that co-teacher teaches them.

Shipped

Exportable accommodation reporting, tied directly to the proof-of-delivery need found in phase 4.

Understanding

A working, functional understanding of special education across the team: classroom models, the IEP lifecycle, who's accountable and who's exposed. Not previously documented anywhere.

Recruitment

Sign-up flow rebuilt to identify SPED teachers by classroom type, not a single yes/no. This is what made every study after chapter one faster to staff.

Adoption

Invite emails and in-app reminders retimed around when teachers actually do admin work, found through a week-long cross-team pulse study.

Timing

Recruitment measurably eased by the next Back to School cycle, the first real evidence the sign-up fix was working.

Reflection

What's still open

The district-level thread was still being pulled when this work was handed off. The classroom and co-teaching mismatch is well understood. What isn't yet is how that same mismatch resolves, or doesn't, at the level where legal accountability actually lands.

What carried forward wasn't just findings. It was a rebuilt recruitment pipeline and a set of relationships with teachers who trusted the research enough to talk about legally sensitive territory. Both of those outlast any single study.
